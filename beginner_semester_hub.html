How a session runs

Every session has the same shape, because at least a week passes between classes, sometimes two, and the first ten minutes are for getting things back.

  1. Retrieval opener, 10 minutes. Two questions on paper about work from one and two sessions ago. No editor until both are written down.
  2. Chunks, 45 to 65 minutes. One idea each. You predict what the code will do before you run it, then you run it and read what actually happened.
  3. Hinge question. One question that reaches back to an earlier session, or to today, answered before the build starts.
  4. Build, 10 to 25 minutes. A brief with an expected and got sheet. The expected column gets filled in first.
  5. Exit and ledger stamp, 5 to 10 minutes. Floor, middle, and stretch are all real landings. One stamp goes on the semester card.

Monday has twelve dates, so the required course is twelve sessions, and Friday spends its extra date on a bonus that nothing later depends on. If a parent compares the two classes, the required content is identical.

From session 4 on, Friday runs one to two sessions ahead of Monday, so in most weeks the two sections are on different pages. Each page shows both dates in its header. If Fri Oct 2 is lost to Sukkot, drop the bonus, move sessions 3 to 11 one Friday later, and keep session 12 on Dec 18.

Machines and libraries

VS Code on the desktop, throughout. One graphics library, pygame, from session 5 onward, installed with pip install pygame. There is no turtle session and no second graphics library, because turtle does not run in our VS Code setup.

What students cannot do at home

Assume no working Python between sessions. Nothing in this course is assigned as homework and nothing depends on work done outside the room. What travels home is the ledger card, on paper.

Standards, in short

Every session maps to at most three standards, and every code on this site was resolved through the Learning Commons Knowledge Graph (CASE Network) on 2026-09-12 rather than written from memory. The mathematics codes come from the New York Next Generation Mathematics Learning Standards and the Standards for Mathematical Practice. The reading, writing, and speaking codes come from the New York Next Generation ELA Learning Standards.

Not claimed here: computer science standards. The New York Computer Science and Digital Fluency standards and the CSTA standards are not in the connector, so no CS code appears anywhere on this site. Add them by hand when you have the codes in front of you, at no more than three per session, footered as mapped to.
